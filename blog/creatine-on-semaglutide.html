Should you take creatine on semaglutide? Here's what the evidence says.

Creatine monohydrate is one of the most studied sports supplements in existence — over 500 peer-reviewed trials. For people on GLP-1 therapy trying to preserve lean mass, the case for using it is straightforward.

What creatine actually does

Creatine is a compound stored in muscle cells that helps regenerate ATP — the primary energy currency of short-burst, high-effort muscle contractions. Higher creatine stores in muscle cells mean more capacity for strength output during exercise, which means a stronger training stimulus, which means a stronger lean mass preservation signal.

It also increases intramuscular water content, which contributes to the volumizing effect (muscles appear slightly larger and fuller with adequate creatine saturation). This is not water weight in the pejorative sense — it's functional muscle hydration that supports contractile performance.

Creatine monohydrate is not a stimulant, not a hormone, and not a steroid. It is a naturally occurring compound found in meat and fish, synthesized in the body, and supplemented to maintain stores above what diet alone provides during caloric restriction.

Why it matters specifically on GLP-1

Dietary creatine comes almost entirely from meat and fish — approximately 1–2g per day on a typical omnivore diet. On GLP-1 therapy with significantly reduced food intake, dietary creatine drops proportionally. Combined with reduced physical activity in some users during weight loss, muscle creatine stores can decline.

The lean mass preservation argument for creatine on GLP-1 is the same as in any caloric deficit: maintaining creatine saturation preserves the ability to exercise with adequate intensity, which preserves the signal for muscle retention. It doesn't work on its own — it works as a multiplier on resistance exercise and adequate protein intake.

The dose question

There are two creatine dosing protocols:

Loading phase: 20g/day for 5–7 days to rapidly saturate muscle stores, followed by a 3–5g/day maintenance dose. Loading causes faster saturation but increases GI discomfort risk — a particular concern for people already managing GLP-1-related GI symptoms.

Maintenance-only: 3–5g/day from the start. Achieves the same saturation in 3–4 weeks without the GI load. This is the more appropriate protocol for GLP-1 users. Anchor Muscle Guard contains 3g — the lower end of the maintenance range, chosen specifically to avoid contributing to GI burden.

Common concerns addressed

"Won't creatine cause water retention?" — Creatine pulls water into muscle cells, not subcutaneous space. This is different from the fluid retention caused by high sodium intake or hormonal changes. The weight gain from creatine saturation (typically 0.5–1.5kg) is intramuscular water, not puffiness or bloating.

"Is it safe to take with a prescription medication?" — Creatine monohydrate has no known interactions with GLP-1 receptor agonists. It is metabolized by a separate pathway and cleared by the kidneys. People with pre-existing kidney disease should consult their physician before use; for healthy individuals, decades of research at standard doses show no kidney harm.

"Do I need to cycle it?" — No. There is no evidence supporting creatine cycling. Continuous daily use at 3–5g/day is the studied protocol.
